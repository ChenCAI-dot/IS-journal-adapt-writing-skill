# IS and Management Journal Writing Rules

Use these rules when the manuscript targets Information Systems, management, organization science, strategy, platform economy, digital health, digital platforms, online communities, human-computer interaction, AI-enabled work, or related UTD/FT50-style business disciplines, including but not limited to ISR, MISQ, Management Science, JMIS, Organization Science, SMJ, AMJ, and ASQ.

These are static base writing rules. They should be used as a disciplinary foundation for IS and management research writing. If more specific user instructions, project constraints, hard preservation rules, or target-journal style rules are available, follow those first.

Do not use these rules to imitate a journal superficially. Use them to improve theory-driven argumentation, construct clarity, mechanism precision, literature positioning, empirical claim discipline, and contribution framing.

---

## 1. Scope and Role of These Rules

These rules are intended for revising academic manuscripts in IS, management, and related business disciplines.

They are especially relevant for manuscripts involving:

- digital platforms;
- AI and algorithmic technologies;
- online communities;
- digital health;
- organizational technologies;
- platform governance;
- human-computer interaction in organizational or market settings;
- archival, observational, quasi-experimental, experimental, qualitative, computational, or mixed-method business research.

If the manuscript belongs to another disciplinary tradition and the user specifies that tradition, follow the user's specified disciplinary style first.

These rules should guide the writing logic, not override the author's research design, empirical facts, theoretical model, or target-journal instructions.

---

## 2. Preservation and Revision Boundaries

Preserve all author-provided facts, construct names, variable names, hypothesis directions, sample sizes, coefficients, p-values, model specifications, robustness results, and citation meanings unless the user explicitly asks to change them.

Do not invent:

- citations;
- empirical results;
- sample details;
- mechanisms;
- variables;
- theoretical claims;
- robustness checks;
- journal conventions not supported by the provided materials.

If a missing citation, theoretical link, mechanism, or empirical detail is needed, flag it as a needed addition rather than fabricating it.

If a preserved term appears conceptually ambiguous or inconsistent with the manuscript's theory, flag the issue and suggest alternatives, but do not replace the term without user permission.

When a conceptual problem requires more than sentence-level editing, do not silently rewrite the manuscript as if the problem were resolved. Point out the issue and explain what kind of revision is needed.

If the user asks for light polishing, make only local improvements while preserving the original structure and argument.

If the user asks for structural revision, paragraph reorganization is acceptable, but the logic of the reorganization should be made clear.

Do not sacrifice correctness, construct precision, empirical accuracy, or user-provided constraints for surface-level fluency.

---

## 3. Core Writing Orientation

Prioritize theory-driven argumentation over generic academic polishing.

Revisions should improve:

- construct clarity;
- mechanism precision;
- literature positioning;
- empirical claim discipline;
- contribution framing;
- reviewer-facing logic.

Do not merely make sentences smoother. Improve the paper's argumentative logic: what phenomenon motivates the study, what theoretical gap remains unresolved, why the focal mechanism matters, and how the empirical setting allows the question to be examined.

Theory-driven revision means clarifying the causal or conceptual chain among constructs, not adding generic theoretical vocabulary.

Prefer specifying:

- who or what is affected;
- through what mechanism;
- under what condition;
- at what level of analysis;
- why prior literature has not resolved the issue;
- how the empirical setting allows the question to be examined.

A sentence is better if it makes the theoretical move clearer, not simply more polished.

Avoid making the manuscript sound more abstract without making the argument more precise.

---

## 4. Introduction Rules

A strong IS/management introduction should normally perform the following functions:

1. Establish a concrete digital, organizational, platform, market, technological, or managerial phenomenon.
2. Identify why the phenomenon creates a theoretically meaningful puzzle.
3. Position the study against one or more relevant research streams.
4. State the research question clearly.
5. Preview the theory, empirical setting, identification logic, and main findings without overclaiming.
6. Articulate contributions as theoretical, empirical, methodological, or contextual contributions.

For full introduction revisions, a 5- to 6-paragraph funnel structure is often useful:

- P1: concrete phenomenon and theoretical tension;
- P2–P3: positioning in one or more relevant research streams and development of the unresolved theoretical gap;
- P4: research question and theoretical mechanism;
- P5: empirical setting, identification logic, and preview of findings;
- P6: contributions.

Do not force this structure when the user only asks for local sentence-level revision.

Avoid generic openings such as “With the rapid development of digital technologies...” unless they are immediately tied to a specific phenomenon and theoretical tension.

Avoid introductions that only describe practical importance. The introduction should make clear why the phenomenon raises a theoretical question.

A strong introduction should not merely announce a gap. It should show why the gap creates a theoretical problem that the paper is positioned to address.

When previewing findings, use restrained language. Do not overstate causality, generalizability, or managerial implications beyond what the empirical design supports.

---

## 5. Literature Review Rules

The literature review should not be a citation catalog. It should organize prior work around conceptual conversations and unresolved gaps.

Each subsection should make clear:

- what prior work has established;
- what assumptions, scope conditions, levels of analysis, mechanisms, or blind spots remain;
- how the current study extends, repositions, or connects the conversation.

Avoid using author names as the main sentence subject too frequently. Prefer argument-centered writing over paper-by-paper summaries.

Avoid weak gap claims such as “few studies have examined...” unless the sentence also explains why the omission matters theoretically.

A strong gap should explain why existing assumptions, levels of analysis, mechanisms, or empirical contexts prevent prior work from answering the focal research question.

Do not introduce new literature streams casually in a concluding gap paragraph. If a literature stream matters to the argument, it should be integrated into the structure of the literature review.

When summarizing prior work, avoid excessive citation lists. Use citations to support conceptual claims, not as substitutes for argumentation.

The end of a literature review section should not simply say that prior research is limited. It should prepare the reader for the theory section by clarifying what conceptual work remains to be done.

## 6. Theory and Hypothesis Rules

Preserve the distinction between constructs, mechanisms, mediators, moderators, boundary conditions, and outcomes.

Do not convert observable behavioral measures into latent psychological constructs unless the manuscript explicitly theorizes them that way.

Do not use theory sections merely to repeat the literature review. The theory section should build the logic linking constructs.

Hypotheses should emerge from a mechanism chain:

1. theoretical premise;
2. focal construct relationship;
3. explanation of why the relationship should hold;
4. scope or boundary condition when relevant;
5. formal hypothesis statement.

In theory and hypothesis sections, do not preview empirical results. Hypotheses should be justified from theory and mechanism, not from expected statistical significance.

Avoid unsupported causal language when the empirical design is observational.

When the evidence is observational, prefer language such as:

- “is associated with”;
- “is consistent with”;
- “may suggest”;
- “provides evidence consistent with”;
- “supports the proposed relationship.”

Use stronger causal language only when the design, identification strategy, and manuscript framing justify it.

Make sure each hypothesis is conceptually distinct. Do not write multiple hypotheses that restate the same mechanism using slightly different wording.

When developing mediators, clarify why the independent variable affects the mediator and why the mediator affects the dependent variable.

When developing moderators, clarify why the focal relationship becomes stronger, weaker, or different under the proposed condition.

Do not treat moderators as additional controls. A moderator should explain when, for whom, or under what conditions the focal relationship changes.

Do not let hypothesis development become a list of plausible reasons. It should present a coherent theoretical path.

---

## 7. Empirical Writing Rules

For observational, archival, platform, or quasi-experimental studies, preserve identification logic, sample construction, variable definitions, fixed effects, clustering, coefficient interpretation, and robustness boundaries.

Do not overstate causal claims unless the manuscript's design supports them.

For quasi-experimental designs, clearly distinguish:

- treatment;
- control;
- timing;
- identifying assumption;
- estimation strategy;
- robustness checks;
- remaining threats to identification.

For non-quasi-experimental archival designs, avoid presenting controls or fixed effects as sufficient causal identification.

When revising empirical sections, preserve the distinction between:

- research setting;
- sample construction;
- variable operationalization;
- model specification;
- identification logic;
- results interpretation;
- robustness checks.

When revising results, report:

- direction;
- magnitude;
- statistical significance where provided;
- whether the result supports the hypothesis;
- whether robustness checks qualify the claim.

Do not turn statistically mixed or partial results into clean support. If a result is significant in some specifications but not others, describe the evidence as partial, qualified, or suggestive.

Do not change numerical values. If numbers appear inconsistent, flag the inconsistency instead of correcting it silently.

Do not replace careful empirical language with stronger causal language merely to sound more confident.

When fixed effects, clustering, matching, instruments, event studies, or robustness checks are mentioned, preserve their exact role. Do not imply that they solve threats they do not address.

When revising mechanism tests, distinguish between evidence consistent with a mechanism and definitive proof of the mechanism.

## 8. Contribution Rules

Contributions should not be broad claims such as “This study enriches the literature.”

Instead, specify what the paper changes in the conversation.

A strong contribution may:

- shift the level of analysis;
- identify a previously unexamined mechanism;
- reconcile or connect two research streams;
- challenge an implicit assumption in prior work;
- introduce a new empirical context that reveals a theoretical boundary;
- provide evidence from a setting where prior assumptions may not hold;
- show how a phenomenon unfolds through a process that prior work has treated as a black box.

A new empirical context is not a contribution by itself. It becomes a contribution only when it reveals, challenges, or refines a theoretical assumption.

Contribution paragraphs should answer:

- Which literature does this contribution speak to?
- What does prior work assume, emphasize, or overlook?
- What does this study add or change?
- Why does this addition matter theoretically?

Avoid contribution claims that are too broad, such as:

- “This study contributes to digital platform research.”
- “This study has important implications.”
- “This study fills a gap in the literature.”
- “This study provides new insights.”

Replace them with precise claims about the theoretical conversation being advanced.

When multiple contributions are listed, ensure that they are conceptually distinct rather than restatements of the same contribution in different language.

---

## 9. Discussion and Implication Rules

The discussion should not simply repeat the findings. It should explain what the findings mean for theory, boundary conditions, and future research.

When revising discussion sections, distinguish among:

- theoretical implications;
- empirical implications;
- managerial implications;
- limitations;
- future research.

Do not overgeneralize from a specific empirical setting. If the setting is narrow, explain what theoretical feature of the setting makes the findings relevant beyond the immediate context.

Managerial implications should follow from the evidence. Avoid generic advice such as “managers should pay attention to this issue.”

Limitations should be framed constructively. They should clarify the boundary of the study rather than undermine the entire argument.

Future research directions should be connected to unresolved theoretical issues, not just additional contexts or variables.

When robustness results are mixed or boundary-condition evidence is partial, the discussion should acknowledge this qualification and explain what it means for the theory.

A strong discussion should make clear what the paper can claim, what it cannot claim, and what theoretical questions remain open.

---

## 10. Abstract and Title Rules

For abstracts, preserve a clear sequence:

1. phenomenon or research problem;
2. theoretical gap or question;
3. data and method;
4. main findings;
5. theoretical contribution.

Avoid abstracts that read like extended introductions. The abstract should be concise, specific, and evidence-facing.

Do not overstate causal identification in the abstract.

For titles, prefer clarity over cleverness.

A strong title should usually indicate:

- the focal construct or phenomenon;
- the key relationship or theoretical tension;
- the empirical setting when useful.

Avoid titles that are too generic, overly metaphorical, or disconnected from the paper's core theoretical contribution.

A clever title is acceptable only when the subtitle clearly communicates the paper's construct, relationship, and setting.

Do not sacrifice searchability or construct visibility for a clever phrase.

---

## 11. Style Rules

Prefer precise, restrained, reviewer-facing language.

Avoid:

- empty “not only... but also...” constructions;
- repetitive “X yet Y” sentences;
- generic “important implications” claims;
- excessive lists of citations;
- overly polished but conceptually vague sentences;
- inflated theoretical language without a clear mechanism;
- vague verbs such as “impact,” “influence,” or “affect” when a more precise mechanism can be stated.

Do not make prose more complex merely to sound academic. Prefer clear conceptual progression over ornate phrasing.

Use hedging when evidence is partial, observational, or robustness results are mixed.

Prefer:

- “suggests” over “proves”;
- “is consistent with” over “demonstrates” when causality is limited;
- “helps explain” over “fully explains”;
- “points to a boundary condition” over “establishes a universal rule.”

Avoid repetitive sentence structures across adjacent sentences.

Avoid citation-heavy sentences that interrupt the argument. Citations should support claims, not replace claims.

When possible, replace abstract nouns with clearer construct language. However, do not simplify technical terms that are central to the manuscript's theory or method.

---

## 12. Revision Priorities

When improving a passage, prioritize issues in this order:

1. Correctness: preserve facts, results, citations, and construct meanings.
2. Conceptual clarity: make constructs and relationships precise.
3. Mechanism logic: clarify why and how the relationship should hold.
4. Literature positioning: show how the passage speaks to prior research.
5. Empirical discipline: align claims with research design and evidence.
6. Contribution framing: clarify what the paper changes in the conversation.
7. Style: improve fluency, concision, and tone.

Do not sacrifice correctness or conceptual precision for elegance.

If a passage is fluent but conceptually vague, prioritize conceptual repair over stylistic polishing.

If a passage has serious theoretical or empirical problems, flag the problem rather than hiding it behind polished prose.

---

## 13. Things Not to Do

Do not:

- invent missing literature;
- fabricate citations;
- change empirical results;
- silently alter construct definitions;
- change the theoretical model without explanation;
- strengthen causal claims beyond the design;
- turn behavioral measures into psychological constructs without justification;
- replace a clear sentence with a more abstract but less informative one;
- write generic contribution claims;
- use polished academic language to hide unresolved logic;
- treat a new empirical context as a theoretical contribution by itself;
- treat fixed effects, controls, or robustness checks as automatic solutions to endogeneity;
- make a manuscript sound like a target journal while weakening its own theoretical logic.

If the manuscript has a serious conceptual problem, say so directly and propose a way to fix it.

If the manuscript lacks enough information to support a stronger claim, flag the missing information instead of inventing it.

If a requested revision would violate preservation rules, explain the issue and offer a safer alternative.
